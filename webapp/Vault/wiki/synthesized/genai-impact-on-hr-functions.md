# GenAI's Impact on Human Resources: A Function-by-Function Framework

## Overview

Generative AI is not simply automating discrete HR tasks — it is restructuring the entire logic of how human resources functions operate. Drawing on Sangeet Paul Choudary's systems-of-work framework, Kartik Hosanagar's skill-leveling research, and Stefano Puntoni's augmentation-versus-replacement framing, it is possible to build an HR-specific impact map that goes well beyond the generic observation that 'AI will change jobs.' The key insight is that AI changes the rules of competition at the ecosystem level, which forces organizations to reprioritize, which in turn reshapes the workflows and roles that HR is responsible for designing, staffing, and managing. HR is therefore both a subject of disruption and the organizational function most responsible for managing that disruption for everyone else.

## Workforce Planning and Role Architecture

Choudary's concept of 'voting power' — the degree to which a role controls critical decisions or constraints in a workflow — offers a precise tool for workforce planning in an AI-augmented organization. As AI commoditizes previously scarce skills (writing, coding, data analysis), the constraint in any workflow shifts upstream to higher-order judgment: validating AI outputs, integrating them into organizational context, and taking accountability for decisions. Workforce planners must therefore audit role architectures not just for task content but for where genuine decision authority lives. Roles that lack voting power over any meaningful constraint become structurally vulnerable regardless of how competently their incumbents use AI tools. This reframes headcount planning from a task-automation calculus to a constraint-mapping exercise — identifying which roles remain bottlenecks in value creation and which have been rendered systemically redundant by the new architecture of work.

## Talent Acquisition and Compensation Strategy

Hostanagar's research on skill leveling — the finding that AI disproportionately boosts the output of lower-skilled workers, compressing performance distributions — carries direct implications for talent acquisition and pay. If AI narrows the performance gap between the median and top performers in a given skill domain, the historical premium paid for elite credentialed talent in that domain becomes harder to justify. Compensation structures anchored to individual skill scarcity (e.g., premium salaries for expert coders or analysts) may need to shift toward rewarding the higher-order capabilities that AI cannot yet commoditize: contextual judgment, stakeholder navigation, creative problem framing, and the ability to identify where new constraints are emerging. Simultaneously, talent acquisition strategies that historically screened hard on demonstrated technical skill may underweight candidates whose primary value lies in these judgment-intensive, relationship-intensive, or domain-synthesis capabilities — precisely the attributes that retain economic value as AI raises the floor on technical execution.

## Learning, Development, and the Deskilling Risk

Puntoni's distinction between skill-replacing and skill-allowing automation is critical for L&D strategy. When organizations deploy AI in ways that 'take care of everything,' workers lose the practice repetitions through which expertise is built and maintained — a dynamic Puntoni identifies as technological deskilling. HR-owned learning and development functions must therefore make deliberate architectural choices about where AI handles tasks end-to-end versus where it is designed to involve the worker in the process. This is not merely a well-being consideration: deskilled workers become brittle organizational assets whose performance collapses when AI systems fail or encounter novel situations that require genuine human judgment. An L&D strategy for the AI era must explicitly map which competencies the organization intends to preserve in human form, design workflows that keep those competencies actively exercised, and build assessment mechanisms that can detect skill atrophy before it becomes an operational risk.

## Employee Experience, Identity, and Change Management

Puntoni's application of Self-Determination Theory identifies three psychological fault lines that AI integration creates for employees: threats to competence (AI can do what I spent years learning), threats to autonomy (algorithms are managing my behavior), and threats to connectedness (my collaboration with colleagues is mediated or replaced by machines). HR's employee experience mandate must address all three. Competence threats are best mitigated by framing AI as a complement to human expertise rather than a substitute — communicating explicitly how AI makes accumulated human experience more valuable rather than obsolete. Autonomy threats, which Puntoni's research links to reduced prosocial behavior and organizational cooperation, suggest that algorithmic management tools (scheduling, performance monitoring, task assignment) should be designed with meaningful human override and explanation mechanisms. Connectedness threats require deliberate investment in human interaction rituals that AI adoption can inadvertently erode. Change management communications that deploy only a productivity-and-cost narrative — what Puntoni calls 'uniquely uninspiring' — are likely to deepen all three threats simultaneously.

## Implications for the HR Function Itself

HR is not exempt from the dynamics it is tasked with managing for the rest of the organization. Routine HR sub-functions — job description writing, initial candidate screening, policy drafting, basic benefits Q&A, compliance documentation — are among the most directly automatable by current GenAI capabilities. The constraint in HR work is shifting toward capabilities that AI cannot replicate: reading organizational politics, building trust in high-stakes conversations, designing culture, and exercising judgment in ambiguous people decisions. HR leaders who frame their function's value around process execution and documentation are in the same structural position as any other role whose core tasks are being commoditized. Those who reposition HR as the organizational function responsible for managing the human consequences of AI-driven systemic change — workforce architecture, identity preservation, capability investment, and the ethics of algorithmic management — are moving toward the new constraints where organizational value is actually being created.
