# The Book Of Why: The New Science Of Cause And Effect

**Type**: RAG stub — full content in vector index, not in wiki

## Abstract

Written by Turing Award winner Judea Pearl and Dana Mackenzie (2018), this book presents the "new science" of causal inference—a mathematical framework for distinguishing cause from effect that goes beyond what data and statistics alone can reveal. Pearl argues that data are "profoundly dumb" and that Big Data cannot answer causal questions without a causal model, introducing formal tools like the *do*-calculus to bridge the gap between observation ("seeing") and intervention ("doing"). The book traces the historical suppression of causal reasoning in statistics, demonstrates the framework's implications across domains from drug development to economic policy, and presents a "Ladder of Causation" that distinguishes association, intervention, and counterfactuals.

## Key Claims

- Data alone cannot answer causal questions; correlation and conditional probability (seeing) are fundamentally different from intervention (doing), as formalized by the distinction between P(L | D) and P(L | do(D))
- Standard algebraic equations are symmetrical and cannot express causal directionality (e.g., pressure causes barometer readings, not vice versa), necessitating a new mathematical language for causation
- The confusion between seeing and doing has produced numerous paradoxes in science and decision-making
- Big Data and "data science" are insufficient without causal models, despite the prevailing cultural assumption that more data solves all problems
- The new science of cause and effect has practical consequences spanning drug development, economic policy, education, robotics, gun control, and global warming

## Topics Covered

Causal inference, do-calculus, counterfactuals, Ladder of Causation, Bayesian networks, causal diagrams (DAGs), Simpson's paradox, confounding and bias, the seeing vs. doing distinction, history of statistics and causation, Big Data limitations, randomized controlled trials, structural causal models
