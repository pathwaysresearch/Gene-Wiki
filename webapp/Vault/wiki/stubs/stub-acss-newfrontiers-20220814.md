# Acss Newfrontiers 20220814

**Type**: RAG stub — full content in vector index, not in wiki

## Abstract

This paper by David Autor, Caroline Chin, Anna Salomons, and Bryan Seegmiller (August 2022) investigates the role of newly emerging job categories ("new work") in counterbalancing the erosive effects of task-displacing automation on labor demand over the period 1940–2018. The authors construct a novel database of new job titles spanning eight decades, linked to US Census microdata and patent-based measures of exposure to labor-augmenting and labor-automating innovations. They find that augmentation innovations strongly predict where new work emerges, while automation innovations do not, and that the creation of new work responds elastically to demand shifts such as the China trade shock. The paper extends the Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) framework to a two-sector model with differing skill intensities to study how sectoral demand shifts reshape the locus of new work creation.

## Key Claims

- Augmentation innovations strongly predict the emergence of new job titles across occupations and over time, while automation innovations do not predict where new work emerges despite being positively correlated with augmentation innovations
- The creation of new work responds elastically to shifts in demand for occupational output — adverse demand shocks (e.g., the China trade shock) slow the emergence of new work tasks in exposed occupations
- Positive demand shocks conversely promote the creation of new work
- Task creation and task automation are treated as endogenous, with entrepreneurs supplying innovations to either augment workers or substitute labor with capital in response to factor prices
- The theoretical model extends Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) by incorporating two sectors differing in skill intensity, enabling analysis of how trade or demographic shocks reshape new work creation across skill groups
