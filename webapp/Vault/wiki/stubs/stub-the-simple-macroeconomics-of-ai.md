# The Simple Macroeconomics Of Ai

**Type**: RAG stub — full content in vector index, not in wiki

## Abstract

This paper by Daron Acemoglu (MIT, 2024) evaluates claims about the large macroeconomic implications of advances in AI using a task-based model that works through automation and task complementarities. It establishes that AI's macroeconomic consequences follow a version of Hulten's theorem: GDP and aggregate productivity gains can be estimated by the fraction of tasks impacted and average task-level cost savings. Using existing empirical estimates on AI exposure and task-level productivity improvements, the paper finds macroeconomic effects that are nontrivial but far more modest than some bullish projections suggest. The paper also considers negative effects from "bad new tasks" enabled by AI—such as deepfakes, addictive social media, and misleading advertisements—which could offset some of the productivity gains.

## Key Claims

- AI's macroeconomic effects can be estimated via a version of Hulten's theorem, linking GDP gains to the fraction of exposed tasks and their average cost savings
- Back-of-the-envelope calculations using data from Eloundou et al. (2023) and Svanberg et al. (2024) suggest macroeconomic effects are nontrivial but more modest than widely projected
- AI impacts work through two channels: automation of tasks and task complementarities, with existing estimates not always distinguishing between them
- New AI-generated tasks can boost productivity significantly, but some new tasks (deepfakes, manipulative ads, addictive social media, malicious cyberattacks) may have negative social value and sizable welfare costs
- The analysis focuses on the US economy due to data availability; effects on other industrialized nations should be similar, but developing-world consequences may differ
