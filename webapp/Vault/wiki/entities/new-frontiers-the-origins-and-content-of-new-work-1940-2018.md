# New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940-2018

## Core Research Questions
This paper addresses three central questions about the role of newly emerging job categories, termed 'new work,' in the modern economy. It seeks to determine the substantive content of new work, identify its origins, and measure its effect on labor demand, particularly in its capacity to counterbalance the job losses caused by automation.

## Methodology
The study's empirical approach is grounded in a novel database constructed by the authors, which spans eight decades from 1940 to 2018. This database links new job titles, identified from the Census Alphabetical Index of Occupations and Industries (CAI), to U.S. Census microdata. The authors also develop patent-based measures to distinguish between labor-augmenting and labor-automating innovations, linking them to occupations using text from the CAI and the Dictionary of Occupational Titles (DOT).

## Principal Findings
The paper establishes that the majority of contemporary employment is in new job titles created after 1940. It finds that the creation of new work is strongly predicted by augmentation innovations and positive demand shocks, while it is slowed by automation innovations. Consequently, occupations exposed to augmentation experience employment growth, while those exposed to automation experience employment contraction. The locus of new work creation has also shifted from middle-paid occupations (1940-1980) to high-paid professional and low-paid service occupations (since 1980).
