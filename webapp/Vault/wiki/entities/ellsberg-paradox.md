# Ellsberg Paradox

## Overview
The Ellsberg paradox is a thought experiment in decision theory that reveals a key limitation of expected utility theory in describing actual human behavior. It shows that people's choices can be systematically influenced by the ambiguity of probabilities, not just the probabilities themselves.

## The Experimental Setup
The paradox is based on a choice problem involving an urn with balls of different colors. An agent is told that an urn contains 1/3 red balls, and the remaining 2/3 are either black or yellow, but the exact proportion of black to yellow is unknown. The agent is then asked to choose between pairs of lotteries. First, a choice between Lottery A ($100 for a red ball) and Lottery B ($100 for a black ball). Second, a choice between Lottery C ($100 for a red or yellow ball) and Lottery D ($100 for a black or yellow ball).

## Paradoxical Results and Significance
A common pattern of choice is to prefer A over B, and D over C. The preference for A over B suggests a belief that the probability of drawing a black ball is less than 1/3. However, the preference for D over C suggests a belief that the probability of drawing a black or yellow ball (2/3) is greater than drawing a red or yellow ball, which implies the probability of black is greater than 1/3. This contradiction shows that people are not using a single, consistent probability distribution. Instead, they exhibit ambiguity aversion by preferring the lotteries with known probabilities (A and D) over those with unknown probabilities (B and C).
