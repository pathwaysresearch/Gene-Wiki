# Surgeon General's Advisory Committee on Smoking and Health

## Formation and Composition
Following a 1962 report by the Royal College of Physicians in the UK, US Surgeon General Luther Terry announced the formation of a special advisory committee to study the health effects of smoking. The committee was meticulously balanced to ensure impartiality, consisting of five smokers and five non-smokers, with two members suggested by the tobacco industry. Crucially, no member had previously taken a public stance on the issue. The distinguished members were experts in medicine, chemistry, or biology, and included the prominent statistician William Cochran of Harvard University.

## Deliberations and Conclusion
The committee operated in a scientific environment that lacked a formal, agreed-upon mechanism for discussing or defining causality outside of randomized controlled trials. Despite this, the text highlights that their "recognition that nonstatistical criteria were necessary was a great step forward." After reviewing the accumulated evidence, including epidemiological data and laboratory findings, the committee reached the definitive conclusion that smoking caused cancer. The seriousness of their findings was reflected in the personal decisions of its members, several of whom quit smoking after the report.

## Public Health Impact
The committee's 1964 report is described as a landmark in public health. Its authoritative conclusion directly led to significant policy changes. Within two years, Congress mandated health warnings on all cigarette packs. In 1971, cigarette advertisements were banned from radio and television. These measures contributed to a substantial decline in the smoking rate among US adults, which fell from a peak of 45 percent in 1965.
