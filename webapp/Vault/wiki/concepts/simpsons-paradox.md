# Simpson's Paradox

## Definition
A phenomenon in which a statistical correlation can be produced between two uncorrelated variables simply by taking an artificial mixture of two distinct populations. The text describes it as a way in which spurious correlations can emerge from aggregation, shocking those who persist in looking upon all correlations as cause and effect.

## Pearson's Encounter with the Paradox
Karl Pearson observed this phenomenon while studying skull measurements. He found that when data for males or females was analyzed separately, there was no significant correlation between skull length and breadth. However, when the two groups were combined into a single dataset, a significant correlation of 0.197 appeared. This occurred because a small skull length became an indicator that the skull likely belonged to a female, which in turn implied a smaller breadth.

## Interpretation and Significance
Pearson considered the correlation generated by combining the groups to be a "statistical artifact" with no biological or "organic" meaning. He believed it was the result of "inappropriately" combining two distinct populations. He used this example to scold those who automatically equate correlation with causation. The text notes that this paradox highlights the more general and critical question of when it is appropriate to segregate data into separate groups for analysis.
