# Ambiguity Aversion

## Definition
Ambiguity aversion is the tendency for people to favor options where the probability distribution of outcomes is known over options where it is unknown or underconstrained. This preference for known risks over unknown risks (or "unknown unknowns") is a systematic deviation from the behavior predicted by standard decision theory and is famously demonstrated by the Ellsberg paradox.

## Demonstration via Ellsberg Paradox
In the Ellsberg paradox, an urn contains 1/3 red balls and 2/3 black or yellow balls in an unknown proportion. Most people prefer a bet on red (a known 1/3 probability of winning) over a bet on black (an unknown probability between 0 and 2/3). At the same time, they prefer a bet on black or yellow (a known 2/3 probability) over a bet on red or yellow (an unknown probability between 1/3 and 1).

## Conflict with Rationality
This pattern of choices is inconsistent with standard utility theory. There is no single probabilistic belief about the number of black and yellow balls that can simultaneously justify both preferences. The behavior reveals a systematic human bias to elect the known probability rather than the ambiguous one, even when it leads to logically inconsistent choices.
