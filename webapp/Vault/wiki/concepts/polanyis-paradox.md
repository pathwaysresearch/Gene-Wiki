# Polanyi's Paradox

## Definition
Polanyi's Paradox refers to the observation made by philosopher Michael Polanyi in 1966 that "We can know more than we can tell." This means that for many complex undertakings, human beings possess an intuitive, tacit knowledge of how to perform them without a formal, explicit understanding of the underlying rules or procedures.

## Examples and Characteristics
The text provides several examples of tasks governed by this paradox, including making a persuasive argument, telling a joke, riding a bicycle, or recognizing an adult's face in a baby photograph. Mastery of these so-called "non-routine" tasks is not achieved by learning rules but through experiential learning, or "learning by doing." A child learns to ride a bike through trial and error, not by studying the physics of gyroscopes.

## Significance for Automation
Before the advent of AI, Polanyi's Paradox acted as a major constraint on the scope of automation. For a traditional computer or robot to perform a task, a programmer had to explicitly specify all the necessary steps, branches, and exceptions. Because the rules for non-routine tasks could not be articulated, many high-paid jobs intensive in such tasks were safe from automation. AI's ability to learn from data and experience, rather than from explicitly programmed rules, is beginning to overcome this long-standing barrier.
