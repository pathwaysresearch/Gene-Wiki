# Common Cause Principle

## Definition
In his 1956 book *The Direction of Time*, philosopher Hans Reichenbach posited the "common cause principle," a strong rebuttal to the adage that correlation does not imply causation. Reichenbach's principle asserts that "No correlation without causation," meaning that a correlation between two variables, X and Y, cannot be accidental. It must be explained by one of three possibilities: X causes Y, Y causes X, or a third variable, Z, is a common cause of both X and Y.

## Limitations and Colliders
The text argues that Reichenbach's dictum is too strong because it fails to account for the process by which observations are selected. A simple coin-flip experiment is used to show that correlation can arise without a common cause or direct causation between the variables. Reichenbach's error was his failure to consider "collider structures," where the selection process itself induces a correlation, a flaw that highlights a common blind spot in human intuition.

## Psychological Significance
The principle resonates with a deep-seated human tendency to seek causal explanations for observed patterns. The text notes that "We live our lives as if the common cause principle were true," and we hunger for explanations in terms of stable, underlying mechanisms. Direct causation or a common cause are satisfying explanations, whereas colliders are described as "too ethereal to satisfy our causal appetites."
