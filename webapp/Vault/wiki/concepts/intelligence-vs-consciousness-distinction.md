# Intelligence vs. Consciousness Distinction

## Core Distinction

The text draws a sharp line between intelligence and consciousness, defining them as fundamentally different kinds of phenomena. Intelligence is characterized as being about 'doing' things. It is about function, performance, and the ability to take an input, such as an instruction or sensory information, and transform it into a meaningful output or action. In contrast, consciousness is described as not being about 'doing' but fundamentally about 'being' or 'feeling.'

## The Problem of Attribution

This distinction becomes critical when evaluating artificial intelligence, as AI may allow for the decoupling of these two properties. The text warns that we might have systems that are truly intelligent but are able to be so in ways that are entirely divorced from being conscious. Our natural human tendency is to attribute consciousness to systems that exhibit intelligent behavior, which poses a significant problem for how we interpret and interact with advanced AI.

## Limitations of Behavioral Tests

Because of this distinction, behavioral tests are deemed insufficient for determining consciousness. While a test like the Turing Test might be a defensible way to assess intelligence (which is about performance), it is considered 'dangerous' for inferring consciousness. An AI system could be designed to perfectly mimic the external behaviors associated with consciousness—to 'walk like a duck and quack like a duck'—without possessing any of the internal, subjective 'being' or 'feeling' that defines consciousness itself.
