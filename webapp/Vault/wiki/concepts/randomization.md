# Randomization

## Definition and Purpose
Randomization is the core component of a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). It involves using a purely random process to decide which subjects receive a treatment and which do not. This procedure ensures that, on average, the treatment and control groups are similar in all respects, both known and unknown, before the treatment is applied.

## Key Benefits
Randomization provides two crucial benefits to an experiment. First and foremost, it eliminates confounder bias by severing every incoming causal link to the treatment variable. This means it breaks the influence of any potential confounders, including those the researcher is unaware of or cannot measure. Second, because the uncertainty in the outcome stems from a known random process, it enables the researcher to precisely quantify that uncertainty using mathematical procedures.

## Historical Context
The idea of a randomly designed experiment was championed by the statistician Ronald Fisher. At the time, the concept was met with dismay by his colleagues, who were horrified by the idea of subjecting scientific inquiry to the whims of chance. However, Fisher argued that an uncertain answer to the right question (achieved via randomization) is far superior to a precise answer to the wrong question (which is what one gets when confounding is present).
