# Regression to the Mean

## Galton's Initial Causal Interpretation
Francis Galton, when studying heredity, observed that the children of very tall parents were, on average, shorter than their parents and thus closer to the population average. He initially misinterpreted this statistical tendency as a physical, causal process, which he called "reversion." He believed it was a law of nature, akin to Hooke's law for springs, that actively pulled traits back toward the mean to ensure the stability of the population's height distribution from one generation to the next.

## The Statistical Explanation
The text clarifies that regression to the mean is a phenomenon of populations, not a force acting on individuals. When selecting a group with an extreme characteristic (e.g., fathers who are six feet tall), their sons' average height will be closer to the population mean. This is a purely statistical effect arising from the imperfect correlation between the two variables (father's height and son's height).

## Common Misconception
The text highlights that Galton's mistake of seeking a causal explanation for a purely statistical phenomenon persists today. An example given is the "sophomore slump" in baseball, where experts invent causal stories (e.g., overconfidence) to explain why a player with an outstanding rookie season performs closer to the average in their second year, when regression to the mean is a more likely statistical explanation.
