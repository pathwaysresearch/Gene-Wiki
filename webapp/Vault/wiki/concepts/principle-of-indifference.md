# Principle of Indifference

## Definition

The Principle of Indifference is a subjective consideration used to justify assigning equal probabilities to a set of possible outcomes. It is applied when one does not possess any knowledge that would lead them to consider one outcome more probable than another. This principle underpins the "classical" notion of probability, which defines probability as a proportion of enumerated, equally probable cases.

## Historical Context

The text attributes the classical notion of probability to Leibniz and its popularization to Laplace, though it notes Laplace never explicitly isolated the principle. George Boole and John Venn referred to it as the "principle of insufficient reason." The modern name, "principle of indifference," was introduced by John Maynard Keynes in 1921.

## Relation to Interpretations of Probability

This principle is a key element of the subjective interpretation of probability, where probabilities are seen as degrees of belief. The text contrasts this with objectivist views. These include the relative frequency interpretation, advocated by figures like Kolmogorov and R. A. Fisher, and the "propensity" interpretation of Karl Popper, which links relative frequencies to an underlying physical symmetry of the system.
