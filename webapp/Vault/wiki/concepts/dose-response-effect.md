# Dose-Response Effect

## Definition
A dose-response effect is a relationship in which a change in the amount, intensity, or duration of an exposure is associated with a change in the risk or magnitude of a specified outcome. In medicine and epidemiology, it is the principle that if a substance A causes a biological effect B, then a larger dose of A will typically, though not always, cause a stronger response B.

## Application in the Smoking-Cancer Debate
The dose-response effect was a strong piece of evidence for the causal link between smoking and lung cancer. Prospective studies, like those by Doll and Hill and the American Cancer Society, showed that heavy smokers had a much higher death rate from lung cancer than nonsmokers—in some cases twenty-four to ninety times higher.

## Significance as Causal Evidence
The consistency of this finding—more smoking leads to higher risk, while quitting leads to lower risk—provided powerful support for a causal relationship. While not definitive proof on its own, the presence of a clear dose-response gradient makes it much harder for skeptics to argue that the association is entirely due to a third, confounding factor.
