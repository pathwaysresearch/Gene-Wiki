# Canonical Model of Production

## Definition

The canonical model of production is the standard approach in economics that bypasses the role of tasks and directly posits a production function of the form Y = F(A^K K, A^L L). In this model, output (Y) is a direct function of the quantities of capital (K) and labor (L), each made more productive by a respective technology factor (A^K and A^L).

## Core Assumption

A central assumption of the canonical model is that all technological change is 'factor-augmenting'. This means that technology is assumed to make either capital or labor uniformly more productive across all of their activities, rather than enabling them to perform new or different tasks. This simplification allows for a more direct analysis of factor substitution and productivity growth.

## Limitations

The authors of the task-based framework argue that the canonical model lacks descriptive realism. Real-world technological advances, such as robotics, do not simply make all existing capital more productive. Instead, they enable capital to perform a new range of tasks previously done by labor. The canonical model's inability to capture this task-reallocation mechanism is presented as a key limitation for understanding the full impact of modern technology on labor.
