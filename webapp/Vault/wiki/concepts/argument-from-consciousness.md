# Argument from Consciousness

## The Central Objection
This argument, which the text notes was foreseen by Alan Turing, posits that even a machine capable of passing the Turing Test would not be genuinely thinking. Instead, it would merely be a simulation of thought, lacking the crucial element of consciousness. The objection holds that a machine must be aware of its own mental states and actions to qualify as thinking.

## Jefferson's Formulation
The text quotes Professor Geoffrey Jefferson's 1949 speech to articulate the argument's core. Jefferson argued that for a machine to equal a brain, it must not only write a sonnet but do so "because of thoughts and emotions felt" and, crucially, "know that it had written it," distinguishing genuine creation from the "chance fall of symbols."

## Connection to Phenomenology
The text clarifies that the key point of this argument relates to phenomenology, which is the study of direct, subjective experience. The objection is fundamentally about the machine's lack of a first-person perspective or awareness of its own mental states and actions.
