# Marginal Productivity Theory of Wages

## Definition
The Marginal Productivity Theory of Wages posits that in a competitive market, the wage paid to a factor of production, such as labor, is equal to its marginal product. The provided text applies this by stating that the marginal product of low-skilled and high-skilled labor to produce one unit of efficient labor must be equal to their corresponding marginal costs, which are their respective wages.

## Model Application
The theory is formally applied to determine wages for low-skilled ($W_L$) and high-skilled ($W_H$) workers in two sectors: a skill-non-intensive sector (U) and a skill-intensive sector (S). Equations A41 and A42 in the text show that the wage for each skill type is set equal to the marginal product of that labor type within a sector, multiplied by the sector's overall wage composite ($W_U$ or $W_S$).

## Foundational Assumptions
The validity of this wage determination mechanism, as presented in the model, rests on the explicit assumption of 'perfect competition in the low-skilled and the high-skilled labor market.' This implies that no single employer or worker has the power to influence wage rates, and wages adjust freely to equal each labor type's marginal productivity.
