# Chapter 4 Summary : 4. Algorithms Become Intelligent

## 4. Algorithms Become Intelligent

### A Brief History of AI

In a letter to Benjamin Franklin in 1783, Hungarian inventor Wolfgang von Kempelen introduced the Mechanical Turk, a chess-playing automaton that captivated audiences across Europe, despite being a hoax with a hidden human player. This fascination with the dream of artificial intelligence persisted until the mid-20th century when Alan Turing proposed the question "Can machines think?" and introduced the Turing Test to measure machine intelligence. Shortly after, John McCarthy organized the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project on AI, which became a landmark event and helped coin the term "artificial intelligence," inspiring significant academic interest.

### Pioneering Developments in AI

[Page 22]
Among the attendees were Herb Simon and Al Newell, who developed the Logic Theorist, the first symbolic AI program, which could prove mathematical theorems. By the late 1960s, Simon advocated that machines would perform any human task within twenty years. In 1967, Mac Hack VI became the first computer to win a game in a human chess tournament. The AI field evolved, with researchers adopting various approaches including rule-based logic and statistical methods. However, neural networks lost favor after critiques outlined their limitations.

## The AI Winter

The 1970s saw disillusionment in AI, termed the "AI winter," as projects failed to meet inflated expectations, leading to reduced funding and public interest. Researchers then shifted focus from artificial general intelligence (AGI) to more practical systems, notably expert systems that operated within narrow domains. Notably, IBM's Deep Blue defeated chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, demonstrating the potential of AI, but expert systems faced limitations due to their rigidity and lack of adaptability.

[Page 23]
## The Resurgence of Machine Learning

As the 2000s began, researchers realized that true
intelligence required algorithms capable of learning from
data. The internet revolution facilitated access to large
datasets, and advancements in processing capabilities and
machine learning techniques fostered a comeback for AI.
Deep learning emerged, leveraging multiple layers of neural
networks to identify complex patterns without explicit
programming.

### Key Examples and Advancements

Examples of successful applications include spam detection
and medical diagnostics. The historical development of a
diagnostic tool like Internist-1 illustrated early Al's
limitations. By contrast, modern systems leverage deep
learning to automatically identify medical conditions from
vast datasets without predefined rules.

### Future Directions and Trade-offs

Both expert systems and machine learning represent
legitimate AI paradigms, each with societal implications and

[Page 24]
trade-offs. The journey from the Mechanical Turk to
advanced machine learning illustrates profound changes in
the quest for artificial intelligence, raising questions about
the future trajectory of this dynamic field.

Humans are complex and unpredictable, raising questions about algorithmic behavior, such as why certain AI respond negatively while others succeed. The origins of human temperament and behavior have been debated for centuries, with various philosophers and scientists attributing influences to either genetic inheritance (nature) or environmental factors (nurture). Studies have shown that traits like intelligence and personality are impacted by both genes and environment, with significant findings that affirm neither side dominates entirely.

# Chapter 7 Summary : 7. In Algorithms We Trust

## Chapter 7: In Algorithms We Trust

### Introduction

This chapter discusses the dichotomy in public trust toward algorithms, particularly in high-stakes scenarios like self-driving cars and investment management. It opens with the tragic incident involving Joshua Brown, who was killed while using the Autopilot feature in his Tesla, raising questions about trust in technology.

### The Tesla Incident

In May 2016, Joshua Brown's Tesla, in Autopilot mode, tragically crashed into a semi-truck, resulting in his death. This incident sparked widespread debate about the safety of self-driving technology. Critics highlighted that Tesla's Autopilot was still in beta testing and the need for drivers to

[Page 34]
remain engaged.

## Public Perception of Algorithms

Despite the potential safety benefits of self-driving cars,
public opinion remains skeptical. Surveys indicate that many
people still trust human drivers over automated systems,
reflecting a preference for human intuition and experience.
Conversely, automated investment platforms like Betterment
gained popularity as people increasingly trusted algorithms
with their financial management.

## Trust in Algorithms: Factors Influencing Perception

1.
**Performance Evidence**
: Public trust is influenced by the perceived performance of
algorithms compared to humans. Many people do not trust
self-driving cars as they believe humans are more reliable,
despite evidence suggesting that autonomous vehicles could
be safer.

2.
**Better-Than-Average Effect**

[Page 35]
: People tend to believe they are better than average drivers, contributing to their reluctance to trust automated systems.
3.
**Complexity of Tasks**
: There is skepticism surrounding the ability of algorithms to handle novel situations, although advances in machine learning challenge this notion.
4.
**Dehumanization Concerns**
: The rise of algorithmic decision-making in contexts like hiring raises ethical concerns about reducing individuals to numbers, which can affect trust levels.

## Observing Algorithm Performance

Research indicates that when individuals observe algorithms making mistakes, their trust in those algorithms diminishes significantly. Instances where human forecasters make errors do not evoke the same level of distrust, demonstrating a bias against machines.

## Implications for Self-Driving and Medical Algorithms

[Page 36]
Following the Tesla crash and a subsequent fatal incident involving a self-driving Uber, public sentiment shifted further away from trusting autonomous vehicles. In the medical field, the introduction of algorithms in radiology has been met with mixed results, as initial systems did not outperform human experts and led to increased rates of unnecessary biopsies.

## Evolving Trust in Medical Algorithms

Recent advancements in deep learning algorithms show promise in medical diagnostics. However, the failures of earlier systems have created skepticism among professionals about adopting new technologies. Some experts believe that, rather than replacing healthcare professionals, algorithms will augment their capabilities.

## Conclusion

The future of algorithms in driving and medical fields hinges on public trust. For algorithms and AI systems to be effectively integrated, overcoming the initial mistrust arising from negative experiences is crucial. As technology evolves, so must our understanding and acceptance of its capabilities

[Page 37]
and limitations, ensuring that enhanced systems are met with
open arms rather than skepticism.
