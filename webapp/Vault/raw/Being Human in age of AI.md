Abstract
The growing diffusion of automation in the marketplace affects consumers in two ways. First, marketing activities such as pricing and customer support are increasingly being automated; this automation of marketing activities has profound effects on consumer welfare and well-being. Second, various aspects or types of consumption activities are also being automated; users may automate elements of writing tasks using large language models (LLMs), they may outsource product search to recommender systems, or they may adopt autonomous features in products (e.g., self-driving cars). This JACR issue on Automation of Marketing and Consumption brings together an exciting set of researchers to explore these topics. In this introduction, we reflect on the role of automation in creating value for both consumers and firms, highlight the themes running through the articles in this issue, and offer reflections on possible directions for future research on consumer behavior and psychology in the face of automation and, in particular, artificial intelligence (AI).

What began with clunky telephone menus and evolved into clumsy chatbots and more or less sophisticated scoring and recommendation algorithms is now moving toward fully automated personalization of most aspects of interacting with customers and managing their experiences. In everyday language, much of this automation is now being referred to as artificial intelligence (AI), although arguably not all of it is AI. Over the last decade, a rapidly growing literature in marketing and consumer behavior has examined consumer behavior in response to algorithms that provide predictions, classifications, or recommendations for, or make decisions about, consumers. Such algorithms, or decision rules, have evolved from conventional statistical models specified by humans (e.g., regression or logit models fitted to well-defined sets of variables) to adaptive machine-learning models that not only describe the relationships between variables but also identify the relevant variables to perform those tasks (e.g., in analyzing and classifying images composed of pixels as data points). Machine learning is a subfield of “artificial intelligence,” a more general term that encompasses computer models and hardware that mimic aspects of human intelligence including autonomous learning, reasoning, problem solving, and creativity, as well as sensory and perceptual processes that provide the input into these intelligent activities. AI is made up of algorithms, but not all algorithms are AI.

We begin by offering an overview of the articles in this issue. Together, they take a flexible view of how automation is conceptualized, from simple algorithms to AI. We broadly subsume the articles into two subsets, one that explores effects that automation and AI can have on various aspects of consumer welfare, the other on how different characteristics of automation and AI moderate consumer preferences for using them.

Marketing and Consumer Welfare
One important motivation for consumer researchers investigating the automation of marketing and consumption activities lies in the desire to explore and detail potential risks for consumer welfare. Firms laud the benefits of personalization that marketing automation can deliver to consumers. They can better tailor various aspects of the marketing mix such as advertising, recommendations, or product curations to individual customers’ preferences (e.g., Arora et al. 2008; Lambrecht and Tucker 2013). Recent research suggests that such personalization may enhance consumer welfare in the absence of price discrimination (Korganbekova and Zuber 2023). Yet research has also explored the welfare effects of personalized pricing, that is, price-discriminating between individual customers. Using data from a randomized controlled field experiment with a business-to-business online recruiting service, Dubé and Misra (2023) showed that such personalized pricing not only increased expected seller profits as should be expected, but it also allowed 60% of customers to benefit from lower prices, compared to standardized pricing. Notably, though, overall consumer surplus declined—perhaps not surprisingly when individualized pricing approaches the theoretical ideal of first-degree price discrimination (Wertenbroch 2019). Taking a privacy angle on consumer welfare, Tomaino, Wertenbroch, and Walters’s (2023) experimental findings suggest that consumers value their private data, which platforms need to implement personalization, less when they barter these data for goods and services—as they do on online platforms—than if they were paid in their cash equivalent of these goods and services. This violation of procedure invariance in measuring valuations reveals a cognitive bias in privacy preferences induced by the medium of exchange (barter vs. cash) and suggests an associated reduction of consumer welfare.

These findings paint a nuanced picture of how various aspects of marketing automation affect consumer welfare from an economic and decision-theoretic perspective. Four articles in this issue of JACR complement this economic perspective with similarly nuanced psychological views of consumer welfare, with the goal either of preventing deleterious consequences for consumer welfare emerging from the deployment of algorithms in tasks previously performed by humans or of highlighting ways in which marketers can leverage automation to increase consumer welfare.

An example is provided by Valenzuela and colleagues (2024), who explore how AI constrains the range of experiences available to consumers. In the realm of digital consumption, where decisions and experiences are increasingly shaped by algorithms, the integration of LLMs and AI is seen as a double-edged sword. While AI promises to enhance consumer life by making decisions more efficient and experiences more tailored and enjoyable—thanks to recommender systems like Netflix or productivity tools like ChatGPT—it also poses risks that extend beyond the often-discussed concerns of discrimination and privacy. This article dives into the less explored psychological tensions consumers face with AI, focusing on how AI might limit individuals’ expressive range, choices, and personal development opportunities. The concept of “possibility space” is central to the discussion, positing that the pervasive use of AI could narrow human experience by imposing experiential constraints through three mechanisms: agency transference (AI’s tendency to restrict one’s exercise of personal agency), parametric reductionism (AI’s reduction of human complexity into quantifiable metrics), and regulated expression (the limits to human expression that emerge in human-AI interactions, and emerging from design choices or technological limitations). The authors call for a multidisciplinary effort to ensure that AI technologies empower consumers, emphasizing the need for a critical examination of how AI interactions during the customer journey may inadvertently reduce or restrict consumer experiences, thereby challenging human agency and motivation.

While Valenzuela et al.’s (2024) conceptual analysis takes a broad swipe at the topic of automation and consumer welfare, two other empirical articles in this issue take a narrower perspective. Duani, Barasch, and Morwitz (2024) investigate consumer perceptions of fairness in price discrimination based on demographic factors, focusing on whether the discrimination is executed by humans or algorithms. The research reveals that consumers find demographic-based price discrimination to be fairer when it is conducted by algorithms rather than humans. This perception is attributed to consumers feeling less judged by algorithms and believing that algorithmic decisions are less exploitative and more justified. Interestingly, this favorable view toward algorithmic pricing diminishes in scenarios involving more common and less contentious forms of price discrimination, such as time-based pricing, and when the price discrimination aims to achieve a prosocial goal. The study emphasizes the nuanced consumer responses to price discrimination practices in the digital age, highlighting the potential for algorithms to reshape notions of fairness in the marketplace. This work sheds light on critical considerations for consumer welfare, suggesting that while algorithmic price discrimination might mitigate feelings of unfair judgment compared to human-based methods, the implications for consumer welfare are complex and context-dependent.

Consumer welfare is a function of choices by firms, policy makers, and individual consumers. Drawing attention to the importance of the latter, Agarwal and colleagues (2024) take a nuanced approach to understanding consumer preferences for automation by examining the role of self-serving biases as a novel barrier to consumer adoption of autonomous vehicles. They demonstrate that consumers prefer lower levels of vehicle automation for themselves compared to what they prefer for others. This discrepancy is mediated by self-enhancing comparative evaluations, where consumers perceive themselves as safer and more trustworthy drivers than others, thereby reducing their acceptance of automated vehicles for personal use. The study underscores the role of self-serving psychological illusions about the self in the context of automation. Under the assumption that adoption of self-driving vehicles will eventually translate into greatly increased road safety, this article therefore suggests a possible psychological hurdle toward progress and gains in consumer welfare.

Novak and Hoffman (2024) take a different tack, exploring a methodological approach that can help managers develop consumer-facing applications as part of the Internet of Things (IoT). This work offers significant implications for consumer welfare by shedding light on how IoT interaction data can be leveraged to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of automation features (exploitation) and uncover new opportunities for automation capabilities and innovations (exploration). Specifically, they argue that by utilizing IoT interaction data, companies can strike a balance between refining existing automation features and exploring innovative capabilities, leading to the development of more adaptive and intelligent automation systems. These systems, in turn, are poised to evolve alongside consumer needs and technological advancements, thereby offering more personalized, efficient, and innovative solutions to everyday tasks and challenges. Novak and Hoffman’s work directly contributes to consumer welfare by enabling a nuanced understanding of consumer automation interactions. This understanding can guide companies in developing more consumer-centric products and services, enhancing the consumer experience with technology, and fostering a more innovative and responsive market landscape.

Moderators of Consumer Preferences for Automation: Process, Surface, and User Characteristics
One of the most important use cases for algorithms in the service of consumers is as aids to decision making. That can take different shapes, ranging from the ubiquitous recommendation systems in e-commerce platforms to decision support systems and other algorithmic inputs into decision making, from sports betting to weather to financial decision making. This JACR issue features several articles that illuminate the evolving landscape of recommender systems and decision support systems through a multifaceted exploration of how these technologies interface with consumer psychology.

Kim and Lehmann (2024) examine how a consumer’s past consumption variety influences their receptiveness to personalized recommendation services. Consumers enriched by a diverse array of past experiences demonstrate greater openness to technologies that streamline the decision-making process by filtering the plethora of available choices. This correlation underscores the pivotal role of past consumer behavior in shaping future technology adoption. Furthermore, Kim and Lehmann’s additional exploration into the impact of decision difficulty adds another dimension to our understanding. They articulate how the interplay between past variety in consumption and inherent decision-making challenges can modulate the effectiveness of personalized recommendation services. For consumers who naturally find decisions burdensome, the benefits of past variety in enhancing openness to recommendation services are mitigated.

Expanding the discourse, Fei and Dietvorst (2024) study consumer preferences for algorithms that appear to mirror the events they predict. This preference indicates a deeper psychological layer to consumer interactions with technology, where the perceived methodological congruence of an algorithm with the nature of the event it predicts can significantly sway user trust and acceptance, sometimes even taking precedence over accuracy. This article is a powerful example of the importance of going beyond a simplistic dichotomy as typically studied in the “algorithm aversion” literature, where researchers compare consumer responses to the same information provided by human or algorithmic recommenders, a topic we will return to in the future research section below.

Together, these studies paint a fresh picture of the contemporary challenges and opportunities facing the integration of recommender systems and decision support systems into consumer lives. They underscore the importance of considering the interplay between human psychology and technology design in the quest for more seamless, effective, and personalized consumer experiences in the digital age. Usman and colleagues (2024) add to this by exploring human-computer interaction through the lens of a fundamentally interpersonal phenomenon: ingratiation. In a series of studies, they explore the effectiveness of ingratiation in AI-driven products and services. They find that AI ingratiation increases consumer acceptance of product recommendations because of consumers’ self-serving motivations to perceive AI’s flattery as accurate and objective. This effect, however, is moderated by the degree of AI anthropomorphism; human-like AI perceived to ingratiate is more likely to trigger persuasion knowledge and skepticism. Thus, while recent literature has mostly shown how humanizing AI leads to more positive consumer responses (e.g., Yalcin et al. 2022), this article points to the existence of exceptions.

Some of the themes in this research are echoed in other articles in this issue. Regarding the effect of humanizing cues, Bluvstein and colleagues (2024) investigate how typographical errors and their correction during text-based interactions affect perceptions of humanness in communication. The results reveal that customer service agents who make, and then correct, a typo are perceived as more human and helpful compared to agents who make no typos or who make typos but do not correct them. The research emphasizes the importance of error correction in enhancing perceived humanness and helpfulness. Thus, in digital communications, the dynamic process of writing, including making and correcting errors, is used by consumers as a cue for humanness. The findings offer an interesting window into our times. They exemplify the enormous technological progress of recent years; to appear more humanlike, machines nowadays need to simulate lapses and human fallibility.

Zhang, Tuk, and Klesse (2024) take a different approach to explore the role of humanizing AI. Rather than examining effects of anthropomorphizing AI processes or output, they point out that the output of an AI is usually at least partially the result of some form of human input. This could be at the level of conceptualizing, programming, or supervision. For example, AI output is often subject to confirmation or verification by humans, a process often referred to as putting a human in the loop (Cohen et al. 2023). The authors show that making the human input in the generation of AI coaches’ advice salient enhances the perceived helpfulness of that advice as it enhances consumers’ perceived understanding of the AI.

Future Research Directions: Which Algorithms
In recent years, consumer research has been enriched by a sizeable number of studies examining the interplay between psychology and consumer reactions to automation and AI. This scholarly discourse has made significant contributions to our understanding of how psychological predispositions influence the interaction between consumers and technology. However, reflecting the lack of a clear distinction highlighted in the introduction between different types of algorithms in the consumer behavior literature, consumer research has typically not differentiated between the various forms of automation in marketing and consumption—from simple algorithms to AI—and has come to use the terms interchangeably. Research participants are typically confronted with an outcome generated, or said to have been generated, by an automated process, which is referred to as an algorithm or, increasingly, as AI, irrespective of how simple the underlying algorithm may have been. For example, algorithmic approval of financial transactions by financial service providers often still relies on simple decision criteria such as a customer’s account balance (Tomaino et al. 2020). We suggest that the lack of a clear distinction has led consumer researchers to focus on the impact of psychology on how consumers assess and respond to automation, primarily by measuring attitudes toward the use of algorithms versus human intervention in marketing, medical, and legal decision making. Much research in consumer psychology has focused on psychological drivers of algorithm aversion, often expressed as a preference for human decision makers over algorithmic ones. For example, Longoni, Bonezzi, and Morewedge (2019) showed that people’s concern for uniqueness shapes their attitudes toward the use of algorithms in medical contexts.

This issue of JACR points to a change of perspective in contemporary consumer research from comparing consumer preferences for automation versus human intervention to focusing on contextual, functional, or surface characteristics of the deployment of AI that enhance consumer attitudes toward specific types of automation. What may turn into the next phase of research on consumers and automation acknowledges that companies will increasingly force consumers to interact with machines instead of humans, limiting consumers’ choice in the matter. For example, due to cost and scale limitations, for firms wishing to provide advice to consumers the alternative to recommendation algorithms is usually not a personalized recommendation by a human expert but no recommendation at all.

We propose that focusing on the specific functional capabilities of AI—from prediction to problem solving to creation—provides another advantage. It enables researchers to examine the feedback loop between human and artificial intelligence, or the way these diverse types of cognition intersect and mutually influence one another. Shedding light on the interplay between human and artificial intelligence holds profound implications for understanding contemporary consumption patterns, which are increasingly the joint result of two intelligences (Braun et al. 2024). As we live an ever-greater portion of our lives online, we are increasingly making decisions in an environment where crucial choice cues are selected for us by algorithms—little of what you see online you see “just because.” Our choices affect the parameters of the algorithms we interact with and of the algorithms that observe and monitor our behavior. This updating process in turn results in changes in the algorithms’ outputs. And on it goes. This topic is challenging due to its inherently interdisciplinary nature, demanding concerted efforts to bridge the divide between computer science and behavioral science, but consumers researchers will fail to provide an accurate account of human consumption in the digital age unless they engage in such efforts.

Future Research Directions: Which Humans
Focusing on specific types of, or capabilities of, autonomous AI may also allow consumer researchers to ask a rather new type of research question—how machines impact people’s understanding of their own psychology, including, for example, people’s perceptions of their own autonomy (André et al. 2018). We speculate that this research direction will become more pressing if, or when, AI systems emulate more and more aspects of human intelligence and even acquire artificial general intelligence. Moving forward, we call for research to begin exploring the potential insights consumers might gain regarding their own psychology through engagements with AI. As expressed in Yalcin and Puntoni (2023, 132), “Now that people are interacting meaningfully with AI and automated technologies, it’s time to switch our focus from what we think about these technologies to how they make us feel about ourselves.”

Thus, AI could be seen as a reflective surface, akin to the protagonist’s portrait in Oscar Wilde’s The Picture of Dorian Gray. AI may function as a medium through which humans gain deeper insights into their own nature and, potentially, perceived shortcomings. For the first time in our history, humanity faces a form of intelligence that sometimes seems to mirror our own in complexity and capability, evoking an encounter with extraterrestrial intelligence. This unprecedented interaction presents an opportunity for introspection and self-examination. This approach could significantly broaden the scope of discussion in the field, opening new pathways for inquiry into the reciprocal relationship between humanity and the intelligent systems it has created.

Consider, for example, how the historical discourse surrounding human exceptionalism seems to have recently undergone a fascinating shift in both popular and high culture. Previously, when people reflected on what makes humans special, emphasis was often placed on cognitive abilities, because the reference comparison category for humans was other animals and cognitive abilities is what differentiates us from them. However, contemporary discussions of human exceptionalism often pivot toward the realm of emotions and social intelligence because the reference comparison category for humans has now become AI and emotional experience is what differentiates us from it. In other words, as machines start matching our high-order cognitive abilities, we increasingly seek refuge in the experiential and the relational. We expect that in both consumption and work situations, people will increasingly value these aspects of our inner and social lives, with far-reaching implications for choices, well-being, and our self-concept.

Automation for Managers and Consumers
AI increasingly allows marketers to automate elements of the marketing process such as decisions around the 4Ps (product, price, place, and promotion) and consumer-firm interactions. Prediction algorithms trained on large and ever-growing data sets can maximize consumer response rates to product offers, advertising and promotional activities; personalize prices; and optimize shipping and distribution in sync with consumption patterns. Many of these automated marketing activities are referred to as personalization. The recent advent of LLMs and generative AI more generally holds the promise of much more meaningful personalization, with obvious benefits for the efficiency and effectiveness of marketing efforts. In addition to automating marketers’ tasks, companies are also investing heavily in the automation of consumption tasks. Across many product categories, innovations provide consumers with ever greater arrays of automated product features. This creates efficiency gains for consumers; in many ways, the automation of everyday tasks is one of the hallmarks of societal progress, as illustrated by washing machines after WWII or by self-driving cars in the near future.

Despite all these positives emerging from the automation of marketing and consumption activities, automation can also create barriers to adoption for consumers. Beyond individual adoption behavior, automation raises a variety of societal challenges and concerns, several of which are highlighted in the research reported in this issue. We hope that this collection of articles will prove insightful to both scholars and practitioners, and that it will encourage others to explore the boundary between technology and marketing, and what it means to be human in the age of amazing machines.
