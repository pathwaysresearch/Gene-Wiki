JPMC – AI Leadership

__Module 1 — Individual: Impact on Work and Workers__

__Theme:__ How to work with AI, and what does it do to the people in the team?

__Session__

__Segment__

__What is Covered__

1\.1 Working with AI

Where AI Fits – Complement or Substitute?

Understand AI's impact at task\-level and role\-level\. Using the prediction\-vs\-judgment decomposition, participants map a role onto an automate / augment / innovate / leave\-alone framework and understand tasks that AI complements versus substitutes\.

This capability boundary of AI is usually unclear and crossing it in the wrong direction costs more than not using AI at all\. 

Through the Jagged Frontier exercise, participants see the frontier directly, examine the difference between passive and cognitively engaged use, and work with two collaboration patterns — Centaur \(Clean handoffs between humans and AI\. Each operating in their domain\) and Cyborg \(Human and AI are enmeshed in the process together\.\)

Impact of AI across the task performance distribution

Skill widening or specialization? Does AI lifts novice performance more than expert? How do these skill shifts change what excellence looks like and what hiring and evaluation should measure? How has the value of different skills changed in a post\-AI world?

Applied to Your Work

Participants apply the session's frameworks to one workflow they own, discuss with their peers and debrief in class\.

1\.2 AI and Teams

Impact on Skills

Explore the “apprenticeship problem” that follows from AI\-induced skills shifts and its implications\. 

When AI absorbs the work through which juniors develop judgment, the pipeline that builds senior talent breaks\. 

How does output homogenization impact team appraisal and performance?

What skills become important for high\-functioning, AI\-enabled teams?

Participants will explore the three things that now require separate and deliberate investment \- domain expertise, working\-with\-AI, and managing AI\-driven work\.

AI and Teams 

Explore how a cybernetic teammate reframes team design\. Participants work through three choices — team size, AI's functional role \(coordinator, devil's advocate, voice equalizer\), and the norms that govern AI use, while examining why adoption fails on culture as often as on capability\.

Ninety\-Day People Plan

Hands\-on exercise to answer four questions\. 

- What changes in how you hire and evaluate given skill compression? 
- What is your one intervention on the apprenticeship pipeline? 
- What team design choice do you want to revisit? 
- What norms do you set?

__Module 2 — Organization: Impact on Process and Performance__

__Theme:__ Do individual AI gains add up to organizational transformation, and what does AI do to the economics of work?

__Session__

__Segment__

__What is Covered__

2\.1 From Tasks to Transformation

Productivity Gap

Understand why task\-level AI gains rarely translate into organizational performance\. 

Using the productivity J\-curve and the complementarity stack \(process redesign, human capital, data infrastructure, organizational structure\), participants examine what separates enterprises capturing AI's value from those still in the trough\.

Redesigning Work

Differentiate between process automation and process redesign\. 

Through a before\-and\-after process scenario, participants apply the prediction\-vs\-judgment decomposition at process level and examine what the AI factory model demands \(data pipelines, algorithmic decision loops, continuous experimentation\) and how new work emerges from genuine redesign\.

Process Your Own

Participants redesign one process they own, discuss and seek feedback from peers\.

2\.2 The New Economics of Work

New Unit Economics

When AI collapses the cost of prediction, the economics of every process built on prediction change\. 

Participants work through the shift in cost, volume, quality, and capability and examine the difference between a cost\-reduction posture and a capability\-expansion posture, and why the former underperforms over time\.

AI and Business Model Changes

Through a comparative case of two firms, participants examine the constraints and opportunities for AI\-enabled business model transformation\. 

Participants also learn about homogenization risk where AI\-assisted organizations can produce less diverse judgment at scale\.

Future of Work

Participants draft a three\-year outlook for their own function, process redesigned, unit economics shifted, business models transformed, new roles emerged\. 

Participants address the questions \- what are the three hardest transitions, and what is the year\-one milestone?

__Module 3 — Governance: Ethics, Risk, and Accountability__

__Theme:__ What does responsible AI look like in practice, and how do you govern it without strangling what AI makes possible?

__Session__

__Segment__

__What is Covered__

3\.1 Doing AI Right

Bias

Understand AI Bias and its five distinct entry points \- training data, label definition, model design, deployment context, and the human\-AI handoff\. 

Through a case, participants apply specific frameworks and examine statistical discrimination versus bias and when each applies in a banking context\. We will also examine the role of geography and task subjectivity in moderating these biases\.

Responsible AI in Practice

Understand the gap between a responsible AI policy and responsible AI practice\. 

Using the RAI Organizational Maturity Model, participants assess their own function across five dimensions \- leadership commitment, cross\-functional collaboration, process integration, measurement, and escalation and examine what responsible\-by\-design demands versus responsible\-on\-review\.

Fairness Decision in Your Function

Participants work through one real AI\-assisted decision in their function and analyze who gets what, based on what input, applying the three fairness notions and specifying what evidence they would need to act with confidence\.

__3\.2 Governing AI Well__

Risk and Regulatory Landscape

AI in financial services operates across overlapping regimes\. 

Participants discuss a structured map of the landscape and examine four layers of AI risk: model, operational, compliance, and strategic/reputational\. 

The goal is pattern recognition across regimes, not memorization of any one\.

Designing the Governance Operating Model

Participants discuss and explore the design challenge of governance fast enough for business, rigorous enough for regulators\. 

Using a risk framework participants design the approval path, evidence standards, and escalation architecture for an AI deployment in their domain\.

Your Governance Playbook

Participants identify 2\-3 AI use cases in their function carrying the most risk today\. 

For each they clarify \- risk tier, regulatory exposure, current posture, gap\. Finally participants decide on governance decision to escalate to leadership in the next thirty days and the evidence to bring\.
